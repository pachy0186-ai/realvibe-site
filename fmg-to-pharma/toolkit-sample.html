Day 1 · Find your career lane

Choose a lane you can realistically enter

Today's outcome: Stop collecting impressive titles. Select a realistic starting point and a backup. Suggested time: 30–45 minutes.

If you have a medical degree but no U.S. medical license, the job market is not one giant category called “pharma.” Site-based research, sponsor operations, regulatory support and data-focused work have different hiring filters. Your first job is to separate the setting from the job title.

Where clinical research happens

SettingWhat teams doRoles to investigate
Research site or hospitalPatient visits, protocol work, documentation, study logistics and investigator support.Clinical Research Assistant, junior CRC, Regulatory Coordinator
CROOutsourced trial operations, data, monitoring and study startup for sponsors.Clinical Trial Assistant, trial coordinator, site activation assistant
Sponsor / biotech / pharmaTrial planning, partner oversight, risk management and delivery.Entry-level clinical operations support, if qualifications match

Titles vary by employer. “CTA” can mean Clinical Trial Assistant or Clinical Trial Associate; these roles may have different seniority requirements.

Check three hard gates before applying

1 · Credentials

Does the posting require a current U.S. professional license you do not hold? Don't assume a foreign MD meets that requirement.

2 · Research experience

If a job requires independent monitoring or years of hands-on trial operations, a medical degree alone does not replace that experience.

3 · Practical eligibility

Review work authorization, hours, location, travel and other stated conditions. Answer accurately.

Patricia's rule: Don't make CRA your automatic first target because the title sounds familiar. Check what the employer actually requires.

Day 4 · Applications in the age of AI

Get past screening by being a genuine match

There is no universal “ATS hack,” guaranteed keyword score, special filename, or secret trick that gets every résumé seen by a hiring manager. Different employers use different technology and screening rules. Your leverage is to meet the actual requirements and make the evidence easy to find.

Eligibility → Parsing → Recruiter or AI-assisted review → Interview

Some employers screen required conditions through application questions. Some parse résumé files or use recruiter searches. Some use AI-assisted matching. A tool's behavior depends on the employer and the feature; not every candidate is automatically rejected by an algorithm.

Evidence-based application checklist

  • Use the posting's terminology only when it truthfully describes your work. If you did IRB submissions, explain your actual role.
  • Show required degrees, locations, relevant experience and systems clearly.
  • Use a simple, one-column résumé with common headings and searchable text.
  • After uploading, check every autofilled field for errors.
  • Never exaggerate work authorization, clinical licensure, or years of experience to pass a screen.
  • Submit through the official employer link and save the job requisition and confirmation.
  • Follow employer rules about AI tools in screening and assessments.

What not to buy

ClaimReality
“I can guarantee a 90% ATS score.”There is no universal score shared across all employers and systems.
“Hide keywords in white text.”It's deceptive and may interfere with parsing or human review.
“Every AI tool auto-rejects candidates.”Recruitment tools differ; some only assist human reviewers.
“A referral bypasses every requirement.”A referral does not erase a hard eligibility gate.

Use AI to critique, not fabricate

Use only public job descriptions and sanitized, truthful summaries of your experience. Never paste patient records, employer-confidential materials, passport numbers, SSNs or sensitive financial information into an unapproved AI tool.

A practical prompt: “Compare this public job posting with my verified, anonymized experience. Separate required qualifications, demonstrated matches, transferable evidence and real gaps. Suggest clearer wording only where it is supported. Do not invent credentials, metrics, scores or hiring probabilities.”

A note from Patricia

I’m a foreign medical graduate from the Dominican Republic. I earned my medical degree at Pontificia Universidad Católica Madre y Maestra, and I originally imagined a career practicing medicine in the United States. The reality turned out differently.

My path into clinical research started on the site side as a study coordinator. I learned what clinical trials look like in real life: protocols, patient visits, documentation, regulatory work, and communication with investigators.

Over time I moved into sponsor-side clinical operations, pursued a master's in Clinical Research Administration at George Washington University, and earned SOCRA CCRP certification. I've managed Phase II–IV studies, sites, vendors, CRO partners and operational risks.

It took years of work. My path is not a shortcut or a template everyone has to follow. You may not need another degree or certification right away. What matters first is understanding which entry point fits your own evidence.

“Residency is not your only professional path. The right alternative still has to be built on evidence.”

Patricia Mieses, MD, MSHS, CCRP · Independent education, not employer-endorsed.
