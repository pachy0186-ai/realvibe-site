10 Career Paths for FMGs & IMGs in Clinical Research, Pharma & Biotech

“Pharma” is not one job, and CRA is not the only route. This guide maps ten distinct career families so you can understand what each one actually does—and why the right target depends on the evidence in your background.

Patricia Mieses, MD, MSHS, CCRPPublished October 16, 2026About 18 minutes

If you are a foreign or international medical graduate exploring a U.S. career outside residency, one of the first challenges is simply understanding the job titles.

Clinical Research Coordinator. Clinical Trial Assistant. Clinical Research Associate. Clinical Trial Manager. Clinical Scientist. Drug Safety. Regulatory Affairs. Medical Affairs. Medical Writing.

They can all sound like different versions of “working in pharma.” They are not.

The central idea

Your medical degree may make you relevant to several life-sciences careers, but it does not automatically demonstrate readiness for them. A better question is: Given what I have actually done, which role family can I credibly compete for now—and what evidence would I need for the role I want next?

First, understand the clinical-research ecosystem

Clinical trials involve several major environments. Investigative sites—such as hospitals, academic medical centers, private research sites and physician practices—conduct studies with participants. Contract research organizations (CROs) perform clinical-development activities for sponsors. Sponsors—often pharmaceutical or biotechnology companies—develop therapies and hold overall responsibility for their clinical-development programs.

A coordinator executing a protocol at one site does not perform the same function as a CRA monitoring several sites. A CRA does not perform the same function as a Clinical Trial Manager overseeing broader study execution. And those jobs are different again from Regulatory Affairs, Drug Safety or Medical Affairs.

1. Clinical Research Coordinator — CRC

Typical environment: investigative site, hospital, academic medical center or research organization.

CRCs help execute studies at the site level. Depending on the protocol and organization, responsibilities can include participant screening and recruitment, informed-consent activities, protocol visits, source documentation, data entry and query resolution, regulatory files, adverse-event and protocol-deviation follow-up, and communication with investigators, sponsors and CROs.

Why an FMG may fit

Medical terminology, disease knowledge, chart review, patient communication and comfort working with physicians can transfer well. What still has to be demonstrated is research execution: understanding protocols, documentation, participant protection, GCP-driven processes and the operational discipline of a clinical trial.

Common misconception

“I am a doctor, so CRC is beneath my qualifications.” For an FMG without U.S. research experience, a strong CRC role can provide evidence employers later evaluate for site, CRO or sponsor opportunities. The goal is not to collect a lower title; it is to build relevant evidence.

2. Clinical Trial Assistant / Clinical Trial Associate — CTA

Typical environment: CRO, pharmaceutical company or biotechnology company.

CTA titles vary, but current U.S. roles commonly involve trial documents plus the logistical and administrative work that supports feasibility, start-up, execution and closeout. Some include regulatory-document review, trackers, trial systems and close partnership with trial managers and site-management teams.

Why an FMG may fit

This can be attractive if you want exposure to sponsor/CRO clinical operations rather than beginning with direct patient-facing site work. It can build familiarity with trial systems, documentation, timelines and cross-functional handoffs.

Common misconception

“CTA is automatically the step before CRA.” Sometimes CTA experience supports a move toward monitoring; sometimes the role develops a different operational path. Read the actual responsibilities and internal mobility structure rather than assuming the title guarantees the next promotion.

3. Clinical Research Associate — CRA

Typical environment: CRO or sponsor.

CRA is fundamentally a monitoring and site-management function. Current U.S. CRA descriptions include site qualification, initiation, routine monitoring and closeout; evaluating protocol and GCP compliance; protecting participant safety; reviewing trial data and documentation; maintaining CTMS/eTMF records; and working directly with investigators and site teams.

Why an FMG may fit

Medical knowledge can strengthen therapeutic-area understanding and clinical communication. But the core hiring question is not whether you understand medicine—it is whether you can demonstrate the judgment and operational skills needed to monitor clinical-trial conduct.

Common misconception

“CRA I means beginner, so I should apply directly after medical school.” Some employers run structured development programs. Other CRA I jobs still expect prior clinical-trial or monitoring experience. Job-level labels are not universal qualification standards.

4. Study Start-Up / Site Activation

Typical environment: CRO or sponsor.

Before a site can enroll participants, essential operational and regulatory work has to be completed. Current site-activation roles include feasibility support, collection and review of start-up documentation, tracking activation deliverables, coordinating with sites and CRO/study teams, and escalating issues that threaten activation timelines.

Why an FMG may fit

This path can appeal to people who are detail-oriented, enjoy documentation and stakeholder coordination, and prefer structured operational work over direct patient interaction.

Important distinction

Study Start-Up is not the same profession as Regulatory Affairs. Start-up sits close to trial execution and site activation. Regulatory Affairs can span development strategy, health-authority interactions and submissions across the product lifecycle.

5. Clinical Operations / Clinical Trial Management

Typical environment: sponsor, biotech or CRO.

Titles include Associate CTM, Clinical Trial Manager, Clinical Study Manager and similar variants. Current U.S. CTM roles describe managing study components or entire studies, defining logistics and plans, protecting timelines and budgets, solving operational risks, coordinating cross-functional teams and, at more senior levels, overseeing CROs and vendors.

Why an FMG may fit

Clinical understanding helps, but this is generally not a first job simply because someone holds an MD. Employers are assessing evidence of increasingly complex trial operations, decision-making and ownership.

Founder perspective

My own path went from the site to sponsor-side clinical operations.

I am a foreign-trained physician who built my U.S. career through clinical research rather than U.S. medical practice. My progression was Clinical Research Coordinator → Senior CRC → Clinical Trial Manager → Senior Clinical Trial Manager.

That path shows that site experience can become a foundation for sponsor-side clinical operations. It is not a universal ladder that every FMG should copy. Your next move should be based on what you have actually done and what your target role requires.

6. Regulatory Affairs

Typical environment: pharmaceutical, biotechnology or medical-device company; consultancy; or related regulatory organization.

Regulatory Affairs is broader than maintaining a site's regulatory binder. The Regulatory Affairs Professionals Society describes work that can include regulatory strategy, interpreting changing requirements, advising development teams, preparing health-authority submissions, communicating with agencies and identifying regulatory risk across the product lifecycle.

Why an FMG may fit

Scientific literacy, analytical thinking, writing and the ability to understand clinical evidence can be relevant. What matters is whether you also have or can build the regulatory knowledge and experience expected for the specific level.

7. Clinical Scientist / Clinical Development

Typical environment: pharmaceutical or biotechnology sponsor.

Clinical Scientists work closer to the scientific development of a program. Current roles can involve protocol development, clinical-study design, study execution, review and interpretation of clinical data, investigator interaction, scientific presentations and regulatory-filing support.

Why an FMG may fit

The scientific and clinical content can feel naturally aligned with a medical degree. But MD does not equal automatic Clinical Scientist qualification. Employers may look for clinical-development experience, therapeutic expertise, trial-design exposure, data interpretation and other evidence beyond the degree itself.

8. Pharmacovigilance / Drug Safety

Typical environment: pharmaceutical or biotechnology company, or CRO.

Drug-safety roles vary substantially. At the safety-science level, current roles include reviewing and analyzing safety data, signal surveillance, aggregate safety reports, benefit-risk and risk-management activities, and contributing safety content to clinical and regulatory documents.

Why an FMG may fit

Clinical reasoning, adverse-event context, disease knowledge and medical terminology can be useful. But “pharmacovigilance” covers multiple kinds of work—from case processing and operations to senior safety science—so target the function and level, not merely the keyword.

9. Medical Affairs / Medical Science Liaison

Typical environment: pharmaceutical or biotechnology company.

Medical Affairs contains several functions. One of the most visible is Medical Science Liaison. Current U.S. MSL roles describe field-based scientific exchange with healthcare professionals and key opinion leaders, scientific presentations, responses to unsolicited medical questions, therapeutic expertise and collection of field insights.

Why an FMG may fit

Advanced medical training can be relevant to scientific credibility and communication. But the degree is not an automatic entry ticket. Current MSL postings may also require prior industry/MSL experience or significant practical experience in the therapeutic area. Medical Affairs should not be treated as the default destination for every physician.

10. Medical Writing

Typical environment: pharma, biotech, CRO, agency or consulting organization.

Medical writing includes multiple specialties. Regulatory medical writers may author and coordinate protocols, clinical study reports, Investigator's Brochures and submission documents while collaborating with clinical operations, statistics, regulatory and scientific teams.

Why an FMG may fit

Medical training can help you understand complex clinical information. But understanding the science and being able to produce high-quality regulated scientific documents are different capabilities. Employers may expect demonstrated writing experience, document standards and the ability to interpret clinical data precisely.

So which path is “best” for an FMG?

There is no single best path. That is the problem with generic advice such as “FMGs should become CRAs” or “doctors belong in Medical Affairs.”

Two people can have the exact same medical degree and need completely different strategies:

  • No U.S. research experience: a site research or appropriate trial-support role may create more credible evidence than repeatedly targeting CTM, Clinical Scientist or experienced CRA positions.
  • Several years of strong CRC experience: the problem may be advancement—identifying which site-to-CRO or site-to-sponsor move the actual responsibilities support.
  • Strong publications and professional scientific writing: Medical Writing or another scientific function may deserve closer examination.
  • Relevant therapeutic expertise plus excellent scientific communication: Medical Affairs may be worth exploring, while still checking employer-specific experience expectations.

Same degree. Different evidence. Different path.

What your medical degree can—and cannot—do

Your foreign medical degree can contribute medical terminology, disease understanding, clinical reasoning, scientific literacy, patient-care context and credibility when communicating complex medical information.

But the degree alone does not demonstrate CRA monitoring, sponsor-side trial management, regulatory strategy, pharmacovigilance expertise, Medical Affairs experience, clinical-development experience or regulatory medical writing.

Transferable potential and demonstrated job readiness are not the same thing.

A better way to choose your target

  1. Inventory what you have actually done. Focus on responsibilities and evidence, not only credentials.
  2. Choose the environment. Site, CRO, sponsor, biotech or another setting?
  3. Choose the kind of work. Patient-facing execution, monitoring, operations, scientific analysis, safety, regulatory strategy or scientific communication?
  4. Read current job descriptions. Look for repeated requirements across 10–20 jobs in one role family.
  5. Separate experience gaps from positioning gaps. A résumé rewrite cannot erase experience an employer truly requires. Conversely, relevant experience that is invisible in your materials may be a positioning problem.

What about salary?

These career families can have very different compensation trajectories, but job title alone is a poor salary predictor. Geography, employer type, therapeutic area, travel, seniority, scope, bonus and equity can materially change total compensation.

The most important takeaway

Do not choose your next move because someone online told you to become a CRA, get one more certification, or apply to Medical Affairs because you have an MD.

Frequently asked questions

Is CRA the best entry-level job for every FMG?

No. Some CRA pathways are developmental, but many roles expect prior clinical-trial or monitoring experience. CRC, CTA, research-assistant, site-start-up and other roles may be more realistic depending on your background and the employer.

Does a foreign MD qualify me for Medical Affairs or Clinical Scientist roles?

The degree can be relevant, but employers may also require therapeutic-area, industry, clinical-development, scientific-communication or other function-specific experience. Evaluate actual postings rather than assuming the degree alone establishes fit.

Is Study Start-Up the same as Regulatory Affairs?

No. Study Start-Up focuses on the operational and document requirements needed to activate clinical-trial sites. Regulatory Affairs is broader and can involve product strategy, submissions, health-authority interactions and compliance across the product lifecycle.

Should I get another certification before applying?

Only if you can identify the specific gap the credential solves. A certification cannot substitute for experience that an employer explicitly requires.
