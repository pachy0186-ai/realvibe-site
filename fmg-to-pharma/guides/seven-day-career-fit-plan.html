7-Day Career Fit Plan for FMGs & IMGs

Before another certification or another 50 applications, find out which roles your experience can actually support.

From FMG to Pharma · Patricia MiesesVideo · 3 min 25 secPublished October 26, 2026

Clinical research, pharma and biotech contain different kinds of work. This seven-day exercise helps Foreign and International Medical Graduates compare two possible directions using current U.S. job postings and evidence from their own experience.

You are building a focused career hypothesis to test, rather than treating a degree, a certificate or a job title as proof of fit.

Your goal by the end of the week

One primary career direction, a short list of evidence you can demonstrate, a truthful résumé focus, and a four-week application experiment. No particular career outcome is promised.

Your seven-day career-fit exercise

Keep a simple notes document or comparison table. Use public job descriptions and your own de-identified examples; do not copy confidential work or participant information into it.

Day 1

Choose two potential career lanes.

Pick two functions you want to investigate, such as site coordination and trial administration, or study start-up and drug safety. Define the work you want to compare, rather than grouping every job in pharma together.

Output: Two functions, with a brief reason each may fit your background.

Days 2–3

Study five actual U.S. postings for each lane.

Collect ten current job descriptions in total. Record responsibilities, required versus preferred qualifications, experience, location, work-access requirements and the level of independent responsibility. Look for recurring expectations across each group of five.

Output: A comparison based on employer demand, with posting links and dates.

Day 4

Sort the requirements by your evidence.

Use three working categories: demonstrated, learnable and not yet demonstrated. For each demonstrated item, identify a real example. For a learning opportunity, state what would establish competence. Treat a mandatory qualification or substantial experience gap as a constraint to investigate.

Something you have not described clearly may be a positioning problem. Something you have not done is not solved by stronger résumé wording. “Learnable” does not mean an employer will waive a requirement.

Output: An evidence map that makes both strengths and gaps visible.

Day 5

Develop an ethical work sample or experience story.

Choose a relevant, self-created exercise using public or fictional material, or a truthful, de-identified story from your work. Explain the task, your own responsibility, what you did, and the result you can substantiate. Clearly label any sample as an exercise, not paid industry experience.

Output: One piece of evidence you can explain without exaggerating or disclosing confidential information.

Day 6

Position the résumé for one function.

Bring the relevant responsibilities and evidence forward. Use terminology you understand and can defend. Keep credentials, titles, decision authority and outcomes accurate; remove claims you cannot support.

Output: A focused résumé version, with every material claim grounded in your actual experience.

Day 7

Choose a direction and run a four-week experiment.

Select the lane with the strongest combination of relevant evidence, realistic requirements and genuine interest. For the next four weeks, target appropriate roles in that function and record applications, responses, interviews and feedback. Use what you learn to refine the approach.

Output: One primary direction and a repeatable way to evaluate progress.

Three common mistakes to avoid

  1. Assuming an MD qualifies you for every role.

    Medical training can be valuable, but each function calls for its own evidence, credentials and scope of experience. Read the requirements before deciding whether the role fits.

  2. Collecting certificates without checking demand.

    First determine which skills or credentials recur in your target postings. A course can support learning; it does not automatically replace required experience or demonstrate that you can perform the work.

  3. Confusing unrelated job families.

    Site coordination, monitoring, trial management, regulatory work and drug safety are distinct functions. Compare duties and decision authority rather than treating similar-sounding titles as interchangeable.

Where ICH E6(R3) Good Clinical Practice fits

Good Clinical Practice provides context for the responsibilities behind clinical research roles. FDA's E6(R3) guidance emphasizes participant protection, reliable results, quality by design and proportionate, risk-based approaches. Read the FDA guidance overview.

For this career exercise, connect your learning to the actual duties in a posting. Familiarity with a guideline or a training certificate alone does not establish independent monitoring, site-management or trial-leadership experience.
